Lars Thrane A/S is a small Danish company in Lyngby, north of Copenhagen, making satellite communication terminals, GNSS compasses and GMDSS radios for ships. Its catalogue is short by the standards of the other makers here — a few dozen items rather than hundreds — and unusually focused: almost everything is either an Iridium terminal or something that feeds one.

## The Thrane lineage

The name is worth untangling, because there are two Danish companies and three related names.

**Thrane & Thrane** was founded in 1981 by the brothers Lars and Per Thrane, and became one of the world's dominant maritime satellite communication makers under the **SAILOR** brand. It was acquired by the British group Cobham in 2012 and is now [Cobham SATCOM](/equipment/cobham-satcom) — so SAILOR equipment is still Danish-designed, from the same Lyngby site.

**Lars Thrane A/S** was founded separately in 2014 by Lars Thrane after leaving the merged company. It competes, in part, with the business he co-founded.

For anyone reading Danish marine electronics history, that explains why two independent companies share a surname and overlap in product category.

## Iridium satellite terminals

Where most maritime satellite equipment uses Inmarsat's geostationary constellation, Lars Thrane builds almost exclusively on **Iridium** — 66 satellites in low earth orbit, which gives genuine pole-to-pole coverage rather than a gap above roughly 76° latitude.

| Model | Service | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LT-3100 | Iridium | Satellite communication system, voice and data |
| LT-3100S | Iridium | GMDSS variant of the LT-3100 |
| LT-4100 | Iridium Certus 100 | Mid-rate terminal |
| LT-4100S | Iridium Certus 100 | GMDSS variant |
| LT-4200 | Iridium Certus 200 | Higher-rate terminal |
| LT-4200S | Iridium Certus 200 | GMDSS variant |

The **S** suffix marks the GMDSS-approved variant throughout the range.

This matters more than it first appears. Iridium was approved as a GMDSS satellite provider in 2018 and entered service in 2020, ending Inmarsat's four-decade monopoly on satellite distress alerting. Because Iridium's constellation covers the poles, an Iridium GMDSS terminal satisfies **sea area A4** — the polar regions — where an Inmarsat terminal cannot. For vessels working Arctic routes that removes the need to carry HF purely for distress purposes.

**Certus** is Iridium's broadband service tier; Certus 100 and 200 refer to roughly 88 kbps and 176 kbps respectively. Modest against Fleet Xpress or Starlink, but the point is reliability and coverage rather than throughput.

## GMDSS radio and GNSS

| Model | Role |
| --- | --- |
| LT-8100S | VHF DSC Class A radiotelephone |
| LT-1000 | GNSS compass and navigation sensor |
| LT-500 / LT-300 | GNSS sensors |
| LT-2200 / LT-2500 | IEC 60945 approved AC/DC power supplies for GMDSS installations |

The **LT-1000** is a satellite compass: it derives heading from the relative phase of GNSS signals across multiple antennas rather than from the earth's magnetic field. That gives true heading with no magnetic deviation, no compass swing, and no sensitivity to the vessel's own steel — which is why satellite compasses have largely displaced gyrocompasses on smaller commercial vessels. Gyros remain where SOLAS requires them.

The power supplies look like filler in a catalogue until you notice the **IEC 60945** reference. That is the maritime navigation equipment environmental standard, covering vibration, salt mist, temperature and EMC. A GMDSS installation needs every part of the chain type-approved, power supplies included, so a compliant supply is a product in its own right rather than a commodity.

## Where it fits

Lars Thrane is worth knowing about as a counterexample to the assumption that maritime communications means Inmarsat. For a vessel operating at high latitude, or one that wants GMDSS satellite coverage without a geostationary elevation angle problem, the Iridium route is a genuinely different engineering answer — and this is one of the few companies building specifically for it.
