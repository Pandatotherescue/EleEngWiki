If you board a merchant ship and look at the radio console, there is a good chance it says **SAILOR**. Cobham SATCOM — Thrane & Thrane until the 2012 acquisition, and still designed in Lyngby, Denmark — holds a dominant position in maritime GMDSS and satellite communications.

The naming runs on two brands: **SAILOR** for maritime, **EXPLORER** for land-mobile and portable.

## GMDSS console and radio

| Model | Role |
| --- | --- |
| SAILOR 6000 | GMDSS console system, integrating the sets below |
| SAILOR 6300 | MF/HF radio, 150 W or 250 W with DSC |
| SAILOR 6200 series | VHF DSC radiotelephones |
| SAILOR 6391 | NAVTEX receiver |
| SAILOR SP3500 | Portable GMDSS VHF for survival craft |
| SAILOR AIS | AIS transponders |

The **SAILOR 6000** is the thing worth understanding. A SOLAS GMDSS installation is not a collection of independent radios but a console: MF/HF, VHF, satellite, NAVTEX, alarm panels and distress buttons wired into one operating position with duplicated power and a documented failure behaviour. That console is what gets type-approved and surveyed, and it is why GMDSS equipment tends to be bought as a system from one supplier rather than assembled from the best individual pieces.

## Satellite

| Model | Service |
| --- | --- |
| SAILOR FB150 / FB250 / FB500 | Inmarsat FleetBroadband, increasing antenna size and data rate |
| SAILOR 4300 | Iridium Certus |
| SAILOR 100 / 60 GX | Inmarsat Fleet Xpress, Ka-band |
| SAILOR 90 | Ku-band satellite television |
| EXPLORER 323 / 325 / 510 / 710 / 727 | BGAN and land-mobile terminals |

**FleetBroadband** runs on Inmarsat's L-band constellation — the same band as the older Fleet and Inmarsat-C services. L-band is relatively immune to rain and needs only a modest antenna, but bandwidth is limited.

**Fleet Xpress** adds Ka-band for capacity, with L-band retained as the fallback. Ka-band delivers far more throughput but suffers badly in heavy rain, so the two work as a pair: the high-capacity link carries traffic in good conditions and the robust one keeps the ship connected when it rains. That hybrid arrangement is characteristic of maritime satellite design, where the alternative to a working link is a ship with no communications at all.

The **FB150 / FB250 / FB500** numbering roughly tracks antenna diameter and achievable rate, and the choice is a straightforward trade between mast space, cost and bandwidth.

## EXPLORER, on land

The EXPLORER terminals are **BGAN** — Broadband Global Area Network, Inmarsat's land-mobile service. They are portable, set up by pointing at a geostationary satellite, and used by broadcasters, aid organisations and expeditions. The smaller units fold to roughly the size of a laptop.

They appear in the same catalogue as the maritime equipment because the underlying modem technology is shared; the difference is the antenna and the stabilisation. A ship's terminal needs a gyro-stabilised dish that holds the satellite while the vessel rolls, which is most of the cost and nearly all of the mechanical complexity. A land terminal can simply be pointed once and left.

## Why the SAILOR position is so entrenched

Type approval, surveying and crew familiarity all favour continuity. A fleet operator with SAILOR consoles across thirty ships has spare parts, trained crew, service contracts and inspection history built around that choice. The switching cost is high and the benefit of switching is usually small, so maritime equipment markets change hands slowly — which is also why a company acquired over a decade ago is still widely called Thrane & Thrane.
